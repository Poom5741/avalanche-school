# Blockchain Foundations Brief — Part A Curriculum (compiled November 2, 2026)

All time-sensitive figures are dated. Stable concepts cite official docs; market figures cite live trackers and will drift — refresh before publication.

## 1. Blockchain mental model (zero CS background)

**Ledger & chain.** A blockchain is a shared ledger that everyone in the network holds a copy of — like a bank's transaction log, except instead of one institution keeping the private notebook, every participant keeps their own identical copy, and the network continuously checks that all copies agree. It is **append-only**: entries are only ever added, like a logbook written in pen, never edited or erased ([ethereum.org — intro to Ethereum](https://ethereum.org/en/developers/docs/intro-to-ethereum/)). Entries are batched into **blocks** ("pages"), and each block carries a unique fingerprint (**hash**) of the previous block — so each page is physically chained to the one before it. Tamper with an old page and every fingerprint after it breaks, which makes rewriting history detectable and computationally impractical ([ethereum.org — blocks](https://ethereum.org/en/developers/docs/blocks/)).

**Transactions & accounts.** A **transaction** is a signed instruction ("send 1 ETH from me to Alice", or "run this program"). Ethereum has two account types: **Externally Owned Accounts (EOAs)** controlled by a private key (a person's wallet), and **contract accounts** controlled by code that runs automatically when called ([ethereum.org — accounts](https://ethereum.org/en/developers/docs/accounts/)).

**Keys & wallets.** Each account has a **private key** (a giant random number — think of it as the only pen that can sign in your name) and a derived **public key / address** (your mailbox number, safe to share). Crucially, a "wallet" is **not a purse holding coins** — coins always live on the shared ledger. A wallet is a **key manager**: it safely stores your private key, shows your ledger balance, and signs transactions ([ethereum.org — wallets](https://ethereum.org/en/wallets/)). Lose the key = lose the account; anyone with the key *is* you.

**Gas.** Every Ethereum operation costs **gas**, a unit metering computation. Users pay fees in ETH because running everyone's code costs the network real resources (compute, storage, bandwidth), and if it were free, spam and infinite loops would clog the system — gas makes abuse expensive and pays the validator who included your transaction. Since EIP-1559 the fee splits into a "base fee" that is **burned** (destroyed) and a tip to the validator ([ethereum.org — gas](https://ethereum.org/en/developers/docs/gas/); [EIP-1559](https://eips.ethereum.org/EIPS/eip-1559)).

**Why consensus matters — the double-spend problem.** Digital money is just data, and data can be copied. If I hold $10, I can try to spend the same $10 twice — once with you, once with a shop. Banks solve this by keeping the one authoritative ledger: whichever transaction clears first wins. Remove the bank, and thousands of strangers must agree on *which* of two conflicting spends came first, without any referee. This "who gets to write the next page, and in what order" problem is exactly what **consensus mechanisms** (mining or staking) solve: they make it prohibitively expensive for any single actor to rewrite the agreed history or confirm a double-spend, because doing so requires out-competing or owning a majority of the network's economic weight ([ethereum.org — consensus mechanisms](https://ethereum.org/en/developers/docs/consensus-mechanisms/); the original framing is Satoshi's whitepaper, [bitcoin.org/bitcoin.pdf](https://bitcoin.org/bitcoin.pdf)).

## 2. BTC & ETH anchors

**Bitcoin.**
- **21M cap:** total supply is hard-capped at 21 million BTC, enforced in Bitcoin Core's code (`MAX_MONEY = 21,000,000 × COIN` in [src/consensus/consensus.h](https://github.com/bitcoin/bitcoin/blob/master/src/consensus/consensus.h)); issuance falls geometrically, so most BTC will never be mined.
- **Halvings:** the mining reward halves every 210,000 blocks (~4 years, [chainparams.cpp](https://github.com/bitcoin/bitcoin/blob/master/src/chainparams.cpp)). Last halving: April 2024 (block 840,000 → 3.125 BTC/block). **Next halving: ~March–April 2028 at block 1,050,000, reward 3.125 → 1.5625 BTC** ([bitbo halving schedule](https://charts.bitbo.io/halving-dates/); corroborating [Binance Square, Apr 2026](https://www.binance.com/en/square/post/312621864982930)). Exact date depends on block-time luck.
- **PoW in one paragraph:** miners compete to solve an arbitrary computational puzzle; the winner proposes the next block and collects the reward (+ fees). The puzzle's difficulty auto-adjusts so a block appears ~every 10 minutes, and rewriting recent history means redoing more computing work than the entire rest of the network combined — so attacking Bitcoin means continuously out-spending the honest network on electricity ([Bitcoin whitepaper](https://bitcoin.org/bitcoin.pdf)). This energy use is Bitcoin's "security budget" and also its main criticism; estimates are contested — the standard neutral tracker is the [Cambridge Bitcoin Electricity Consumption Index](https://www.ccaf.io/cbesi). Post-halving, miner revenue is ~3.125 BTC/block plus transaction fees.

**Ethereum.**
- **The Merge:** Ethereum switched from proof-of-work to proof-of-stake on **September 15, 2022**, cutting energy use by **~99.95%** ([ethereum.org — The Merge](https://ethereum.org/en/roadmap/merge/)).
- **PoS mechanics (vocabulary your learners need):** validators stake **32 ETH** as a deposit and run validation software; dishonesty gets their stake **slashed** (destroyed). Time is divided into **slots of 12 seconds** and **epochs of 32 slots (~6.4 min)**; one validator is randomly picked per slot to propose a block, others vote ("attest"). A transaction is **finalized** after two epochs are justified by a two-thirds supermajority of stake (~13 minutes) — finalized history cannot revert without burning ≥⅓ of all staked ETH ([ethereum.org — proof-of-stake](https://ethereum.org/en/developers/docs/consensus-mechanisms/pos/)). Since the Shanghai upgrade, validators can hold up to 2,048 ETH each; anyone can stake via pools with as little as 0.01 ETH ([ethereum.org — staking](https://ethereum.org/en/staking/)).
- **"World computer":** Ethereum is a globally shared computer whose programs (smart contracts) run exactly as written, identically on thousands of nodes, with no downtime or owner — anyone can deploy code that anyone can call ([ethereum.org — smart contracts](https://ethereum.org/en/smart-contracts/)). The phrase is a common community framing rather than a formal spec.
- **BTC vs ETH as assets:** BTC's narrative is **"digital gold"** — fixed supply, simplest possible use-case, valued as scarce, apolitical money. ETH is the **programmable platform asset**: it is the fuel for paying gas, the collateral that secures the network via staking (earning yield), and the base money of the app economy. These narratives overlap but imply different risks: BTC bets on monetary scarcity; ETH bets on usage of the platform. Label both as *narratives*, not facts.
- **2026 context (refresh before teaching):** **~36% of all ETH is staked (~43.8M ETH, ~870k validators)** as of late September 2026 ([beaconcha.in staked-ETH chart](https://beaconcha.in/charts/staked_ether) for live data; [KuCoin Research, Oct 2026](https://www.kucoin.com/blog/ethereum-staking-in-2026-why-record-eth-deposits-aren-t-lifting-yields); the 30% milestone was crossed in early Feb 2026 per [Chainlabo](https://www.chainlabo.com/blog/ethereum-staking-rate-30-percent-2026-security-settlement-layer)). **BTC dominance ~59–60% as of Oct–Nov 2026**, having trended up through 2025–26 (roughly low-50s% in early 2025); figures vary by source and snapshot — use [CoinMarketCap's dominance chart](https://coinmarketcap.com/charts/bitcoin-dominance/) live. Total crypto market cap ~$2.6T (Nov 2026, [CoinMarketCap](https://coinmarketcap.com/)). ⚠️ UNCERTAIN: dominance readings ranged 57–63% across sources this fall; treat as "~60%, fluctuating."

## 3. Consensus + ZK vocabulary (awareness level, no math)

**PoW vs PoS tradeoffs.** PoW buys security with real-world electricity; it's battle-tested since 2009 and hardware failure costs nothing but wasted energy, but it concentrates mining in industrial farms and consumes a lot of power. PoS buys security with locked capital that can be destroyed; it cuts energy ~99.9%, lowers the hardware barrier (a laptop can validate), and enables faster hard finality, but is younger and operationally more complex ([ethereum.org — PoS vs PoW](https://ethereum.org/en/developers/docs/consensus-mechanisms/pos/pos-vs-pow/)). Note for balance: Bitcoiners argue PoS's "nothing-at-stake" and governance properties weaken it — this remains genuinely **contested**; present as tradeoffs.

**Finality.** A transaction is *final* when it can no longer be reversed. In PoW there's never absolute certainty — only exponentially decreasing probability (hence "wait 6 confirmations"). Ethereum PoS has explicit finality (~13 minutes, per above). Many L2s inherit or approximate it.

**Rollups.** A rollup is an L2 that **executes transactions off Ethereum but posts the transaction data back to Ethereum L1**, so the data is as available and censorship-resistant as L1 while fees drop 10–100× ([ethereum.org — scaling](https://ethereum.org/en/developers/docs/scaling/)). Two families:
- **Optimistic rollups** assume results are valid ("innocent until proven guilty") and run a **challenge window (~7 days)** during which anyone can submit a **fraud proof** showing an invalid result, getting the bad operator's bond slashed ([ethereum.org — optimistic rollups](https://ethereum.org/en/developers/docs/scaling/optimistic-rollups/); "challenge period lasting roughly seven days" is the page's own wording). Example implementations: **Arbitrum, Optimism** ([ethereum.org L2 networks](https://ethereum.org/en/layer-2/networks/)). Consequence learners will hit in practice: moving funds back to L1 typically means waiting ~7 days (or using a liquidity provider for a fee).
- **ZK-rollups** post a **validity proof** with every batch; the L1 contract cryptographically verifies the proof and accepts the batch instantly — no challenge window needed. Examples: **zkSync ([zksync.io](https://zksync.io)), Starknet ([starknet.io](https://www.starknet.io)), Linea ([linea.build](https://linea.build)), Scroll ([scroll.io](https://scroll.io))**.

**What a validity proof IS (one honest paragraph).** A validity proof (zero-knowledge proof) is a way for one party to *prove a computation was done correctly* without the verifier re-doing the computation — like a tamper-evident math receipt: the prover crunches thousands of transactions off-chain, then produces a small certificate that, if even one transaction were mis-executed, could not have been generated. The L1 smart contract checks this certificate in milliseconds; if it verifies, the result is as trustworthy as having re-executed everything. "Zero-knowledge" technically means the proof can hide the underlying data while still proving the claim — a feature rollups mostly don't use (they publish data on L1), but privacy tech does. ([ethereum.org — ZK-rollups](https://ethereum.org/en/developers/docs/scaling/zk-rollups/))

**Validium / volition (one-liners).** A **validium** is a ZK-rollup variant that keeps the proof but stores data *off-chain* — cheaper, but you now trust a data-availability committee ([ethereum.org — validium](https://ethereum.org/en/developers/docs/scaling/validium/)). **Volition** lets users choose per-transaction between rollup (on-chain data) and validium (off-chain data) modes — a StarkEx/Starknet concept ([StarkWare docs](https://docs.starkware.co/starkex-volition)).

**Why ZK matters beyond rollups.** Proofs let you prove a statement about hidden data: prove you're over 18 without revealing your birthday, prove a company holds enough reserves to cover customer balances without publishing the full balance sheet (proof of reserves), and private payments/transfers. Ethereum's own roadmap lists privacy via ZK as a goal ([ethereum.org — privacy roadmap](https://ethereum.org/en/roadmap/privacy/)).

## 4. EVM + ERC standards

**EVM compatibility.** The **Ethereum Virtual Machine** is Ethereum's computation engine — the sandboxed computer that executes smart-contract bytecode ([ethereum.org — EVM](https://ethereum.org/en/developers/docs/evm/)). A chain that is "EVM-compatible" implements that same execution environment, so a contract compiled once can be deployed near-unchanged on Arbitrum, Base, Polygon, etc., and the same wallets, libraries, and audits work everywhere. This is why the multi-chain landscape mostly speaks one language.

**Token standards** (contracts agree to the same function names so wallets/marketplaces support any token automatically):
- **ERC-20 — fungible tokens** (identical, divisible units). Example: stablecoins like **USDC/USDT** are ERC-20s. ([EIP-20](https://eips.ethereum.org/EIPS/eip-20))
- **ERC-721 — NFTs** (each token unique, non-divisible). Example: **ENS domain names** (each `.eth` name is an ERC-721) and digital art/collections. ([EIP-721](https://eips.ethereum.org/EIPS/eip-721))
- **ERC-1155 — semi-fungible** ("multi-token": one contract mints many token types, each ID fungible within itself). Built for games — e.g., 100 identical swords + 1 legendary sword in one contract. Authors are from gaming company Enjin. ([EIP-1155](https://eips.ethereum.org/EIPS/eip-1155))
- **ERC-4337 — account abstraction.** Makes **smart accounts** (contracts as wallets) work without protocol changes, via a mempool handled by **bundlers** (operators who package many users' operations into one transaction) and **paymasters** (entities that sponsor gas). Problem it solves: normal EOAs are all-or-nothing single-key accounts; smart accounts enable **batching** (approve+swap in one click), **gasless UX** (pay fees in the token being traded, or app-sponsored), and **custom login/recovery** (passkeys, social recovery) ([EIP-4337](https://eips.ethereum.org/EIPS/eip-4337); overview at [ethereum.org — accounts](https://ethereum.org/en/developers/docs/accounts/#smart-contract-wallets)).
- **ERC-7702 / EIP-7702:** verified caveat — the standard is formally **EIP-7702, "Set Code for EOAs" (Status: Final, Standards Track: Core)**; "ERC-7702" is the common colloquial name, but there is no separate ERC-7702 document. It lets a regular EOA permanently adopt smart-contract code via a signed authorization, bringing 4337-style powers (batching, sponsorship, sub-key permissions) to *existing* wallet addresses ([EIP-7702](https://eips.ethereum.org/EIPS/eip-7702)). It went live with Ethereum's **Pectra upgrade, May 7, 2025** ([ethereum.org — Pectra](https://ethereum.org/en/roadmap/pectra/)).

## 5. DeFi + RWA primers (use-case landscape only)

- **AMM (automated market maker):** instead of an order book, a smart contract holds a pool of two tokens and prices them by the formula **x·y = k** — trades shift the reserves along a constant-product curve, so price is set by arithmetic, not a market maker ([Uniswap v2 whitepaper](https://uniswap.org/whitepaper.pdf)).
- **Lending/liquidation:** deposit collateral to borrow against it; if the collateral's value falls below a threshold, anyone can **liquidate** it (repay your loan, take discounted collateral) to keep the protocol solvent (overview: [ethereum.org — DeFi](https://ethereum.org/en/defi/)).
- **Liquid staking:** stake ETH via a protocol that gives you a tradable receipt token (e.g., stETH) — your ETH keeps earning staking yield while remaining usable elsewhere ([Lido docs](https://docs.lido.fi/)). **Restaking:** re-use that staked ETH as security collateral for additional services to earn extra yield (EigenLayer, [eigenlayer.xyz](https://www.eigenlayer.xyz)) — higher returns, higher risk; describe as such.
- **RWA (real-world assets):** representing off-chain financial instruments on-chain. **Tokenized treasuries/money-market funds** give holders a yield-bearing token backed by T-bills — e.g., BlackRock's **BUIDL** fund, issued via Securitize, the largest such product (~$2.1–2.8B in late 2026) ([securitize.io/blackrock/buidl](https://securitize.io/blackrock/buidl); live data [rwa.xyz](https://www.rwa.xyz)). ⚠️ UNCERTAIN: the total tokenized-Treasury market is roughly **$9–10B** across ~60 products (sources conflict: [rwa.xyz-derived figure](https://stablecoininsider.org/real-world-asset-tokenization/) ~$8.9B vs "over $10B by Jan 2026" per [CryptoSlate](https://cryptoslate.com/blackrock-just-lost-control-of-the-10b-tokenized-treasury-market-to-circle-for-one-simple-mechanical-reason/)); check rwa.xyz live. **Why institutions care:** settlement in minutes instead of T+1/T+2, markets that never close (24/7/365), and fractionalization (small units of large instruments) — framing per [ethereum.org enterprise tokenization page](https://ethereum.org/en/organizations/enterprise/tokenization/).
- **Stablecoins:** tokens pegged to a currency. **Fiat-backed** (USDT, USDC) hold reserves of dollars/T-bills with an issuer; **crypto-backed** (e.g., DAI) lock crypto collateral in contracts, often over-collateralized. They're arguably crypto's clearest product-market fit — dollar access, instant settlement, and programmability for the ~90% of the world without reliable dollar banking — with the market at **~$295–300B as of Nov 2026** (peaked ~$322B May 2026; USDT ~60% share) ([DefiLlama stablecoins](https://defillama.com/stablecoins)). For learners: the peg is a promise, not a law of nature — issuer quality and reserves matter.
